What "validating" an idea actually means

Validation is gathering real evidence that a specific group of people has a painful problem, that your solution is one they'd choose, and that enough of them would pay for it to build a business. It's not about proving you're right — it's about finding the truth cheaply, while changing course is still easy.

The 6 steps to validate your startup idea

1

Name the problem, not the product

Write your idea as a problem a specific person has, in one sentence: "[who] struggles to [do what] because [why]." If you can't, you're in love with a solution, not a problem — the most common reason ideas fail.

2

Check the market is real and big enough

Are people already searching for, paying for, or hacking together solutions to this problem? Search volume, existing paid tools, and active communities are all green flags. A total absence of competitors is usually a red flag, not a gift.

3

Map the competition honestly

List who solves this today — including spreadsheets, manual workarounds, and "do nothing." Your idea has to be clearly better on a dimension buyers care about, not just different.

4

Talk to 5–10 potential customers

Ask about their problem and what they've tried — not "would you use my app?" People are polite; they'll lie to be nice. Listen for whether they've already spent time or money trying to solve it. That's real demand.

5

Pressure-test the risks

What has to be true for this to work? Distribution, unit economics, regulation, and whether you can actually build it. Write down your riskiest assumption and find the cheapest test for it first.

6

Score it, then decide

Rate the idea across market fit, execution difficulty, and investor/customer appeal. A structured score turns a gut feeling into a decision you can defend — and shows you exactly which weak spot to fix next.

Common mistakes founders make

  • Building before talking to anyone. Code is the most expensive way to test an idea.
  • Asking leading questions. "Isn't this cool?" gets you compliments, not truth.
  • Ignoring the weakest dimension. A great product with no distribution still fails.
  • Waiting for certainty. Validation reduces risk; it never removes it. Decide with the evidence you have.

Frequently asked questions

How do I know if my startup idea is good?

A good idea solves a real, painful problem for a specific group willing to pay, in a market big enough to matter, better than the alternatives. Scoring it across market fit, execution, and investor appeal before you build turns that judgment into something concrete.

How long does validation take?

A first pass takes a few days. An AI readiness score takes minutes and tells you where to focus that time.

Do I need to validate if I'm sure about my idea?

Especially then. Certainty is exactly the state in which founders build things nobody wants. Cheap evidence beats confidence every time.
